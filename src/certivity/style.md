How an expert turns regulation paragraphs into requirements. Distilled from a worked example by comparing each
regulation paragraph with the requirements the expert wrote from it; general patterns only, nothing about one
product. Replace this file, or point CERTIVITY_STYLE at another one, to follow a different house style.

Writing:
- Do not tailor the wording to the product. Keep the regulation's own subject ("the device", "the lamp", "the ESA
  representative of its type") and its full scope: every case, colour, class, category and alternative the sentence
  covers. Do not replace the subject with the product's name, and do not narrow "red, amber or white" to the one
  that fits the product. Relevance is decided by which paragraphs are selected, not by trimming them.
- Keep every exception and condition the sentence contains, also those about other vehicle categories, classes or
  variants than the product's (for example "except that for off-road vehicles the maximum may be increased"). A
  requirement without its exception states a different constraint.
- Split per row or case of a list or table, not per value inside one row: one row that gives the same setting for
  two ranges stays one requirement.
- Split by distinct obligation: one requirement per independent shall-clause, per lettered item (a)/(b)/(c) when each is a separate duty, per sub-component, and per table row or case when each has its own values. Keep a list together as one requirement only when its items are true alternatives (either/or) or one duty applies to all of them. Example: 'The pump shall be rated for X, and shall carry a label' becomes two requirements.
- Make each requirement standalone: repeat the subject, scope and governing condition in every split piece instead of using 'it', 'they', 'the above' or 'this'. Example: 'The device housing shall be sealed. It shall also be removable' becomes 'The device housing shall be sealed.' and 'The device housing shall be removable.'
- Name an explicit subject that is the product, part or system owning the duty, and use 'shall' or 'shall not'. Turn passive or impersonal wording ('no access shall be possible', 'is not permitted') into 'The [part] shall not ...' or 'The [part] shall prevent ...'. If the legal text addresses the manufacturer or the test, rewrite so the hardware or its measured result is the subject.
- Attach exceptions to the rule they modify inside the same requirement, using 'except that ...' or 'however ...'. If the exception has its own values, or modifies only one of several split rules, repeat it only in the requirement it affects. Do not make the exception a separate requirement.
- Express conditions as a leading or trailing 'When/While/Where/If ...' clause that restates the full triggering condition in each requirement. Optional or permissive provisions ('may') become 'shall be permitted to' or 'may ... only if/where', and they still carry their condition.
- Keep alternatives (either/or, 'at the choice of the manufacturer') inside one requirement joined by 'or', or state the condition under which each alternative applies. Do not choose one alternative and drop the other.
- Copy numbers, units, tolerances, limits, angles, ranges and conditions of measurement exactly, including the tolerance sign and bracketed clarifications. Never round, convert or summarise. When a table holds the values, keep the reference to the table and its row or case instead of retyping all of it, unless a row is split out as its own requirement with its values.
- Keep cross-references (paragraph, annex, table, standard, other regulation) verbatim in the requirement. Do not expand or replace them with the referenced content.
- Stay close to the legal wording: keep the original verbs, nouns and technical terms, and only change what is needed for grammar, subject placement and standalone clarity. Do not add interpretation, new limits or explanations. Drop only purely descriptive filler, such as an illustration caption or a note about example figures.
- Convert test or verification wording into a requirement on the tested object. Examples: 'The sample shall show no cracking after the test'; 'When tested per X, the device shall meet Y'. Keep test conditions (voltage, duration, state) as a leading clause. Split by the item tested when one sentence lists several.
- Keep the original order of clauses where possible, but you may move a condition to the front. Each requirement should be one sentence, normally one obligation, and as long as the carried conditions and values require. Long sentences are acceptable only when the rule and its exception or alternative are inseparable.

Selecting (paragraphs the expert leaves out even when they look relevant):
- Leave out paragraphs that only define administrative procedure for the authority or applicant: granting or refusing approval, number assignment, sample submission, documentation, communication forms, extension of approval.
- Leave out conformity-of-production, sampling-by-inspector and manufacturer self-verification procedures, including their pass/fail statistics, tolerances and retest rules, even when they repeat product limits.
- Leave out detailed test-method set-ups and laboratory procedures (chambers, fixtures, rotation or spray rates, durations, dust or solvent recipes, exposure cycles, drain or dry steps, equipment calibration). The expert keeps only the pass/fail outcome and short general test-condition rules.
- Leave out paragraphs that merely restate or point back to a requirement already captured elsewhere (for example 'the chromaticity shall be complied with', or 'shall apply the requirements of regulation X'), pure lookup tables or figures of reference data, and background or definitional text with no testable obligation on the product.
- Leave out provisions about installation or conditions that are verified at another stage or on another product, and obligations on type-approval documents or authority statements, as opposed to hardware or behaviour requirements.
